Relationship Between Workout Intensity and Change of Energy

Question

Does high intensity exercise deplete my daily energy or recharge it?

Hypothesis

As Active Kilocalories increase on any given day, the energy change will more frequently show a negative result.

In addition, longer workout Duration and high Maximum Heart Rate might amplify the overall effort, leading to a loss of Energy.

Data Collection

The data comes from a personal record collected during workout sessions throughout 7 weeks. Data was recorded with a frequency of every workout session (approximately 5 times per week). This volume of data was sufficient to identify dispersion patterns.

View Data Collection PDF

Data Encoding

  • X-Axis: Date/Day (Temporal/Sequential)
  • Y-Axis: Active Kilocalories (Quantitative - Intensity)
  • Color: Max Heart Rate in BPM (Low to High Intensity)
  • Shape (Glyph): Energy Change (Morning workout vs Post workout)
    • Energy Gain (Triangle ▲).
    • Energy Loss (Triangle ▼).
    • No Change in Energy (Circle ●).
  • Size: Duration in min (Quantitative)

Justification (Analysis)

This proposal is designed to check for sequential patterns and cumulative effects. By using Date on the X-Axis, we can see if a string of high-intensity workouts (high Y-values, large size, hot color) immediately precedes a day where the energy level shape changes after the workout. The use of Shape for the change of Energy Level provides a clear categorical distinction of the primary result.

Conclusion: Energy is Multifactorial

After analyzing the visualization, the initial hypothesis that "higher intensity equals greater exhaustion" has been refuted. The data shows no direct linear correlation: high intensity sessions (dark red dots and high on the Y-axis) result in both energy gains and losses randomly.

However, an interesting pattern was observed: medium intensity workouts tend to cluster more frequently as "Energy Gain". This suggests an alternative hypothesis: moderate exercise is the "sweet spot" for daily vitality. The dispersion in extreme data indicates that daily energy is multifactorial and likely depends more on external variables not measured in this study, such as sleep quality and nutrition, than on the exercise itself.
